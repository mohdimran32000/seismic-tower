I designed a concrete office tower for a strong-earthquake region, then shook it with the real 1940 El Centro recording, stronger each time, until it collapsed. This page shows the numbers behind the video and how they were made.

The building

A 20-storey office tower, 71 m tall, on a 30 x 30 m plan. The earthquake is resisted by reinforced concrete moment frames around the perimeter: 5 bays of 6 m on each face. The interior columns and the service core carry only gravity.

I sized the members with a simplified version of the US code process (ASCE 7 for loads, ACI 318 for concrete) for a strong-earthquake site. Nothing is borrowed from a real building.

2

The earthquake

The 1940 El Centro earthquake in California was one of the first strong shakings ever recorded by an instrument. Engineers still use the recording today. It has two horizontal directions, and I used both: north-south on one axis of the tower and east-west on the other.

To make the shaking stronger I multiply the whole recording. At 2x, every moment of the ground motion is twice as strong as it was in 1940. That keeps the real rhythm of the earthquake and only turns up its strength.

3

How the analysis works

The analysis runs in OpenSees, an open-source earthquake engineering program. Each direction of the tower is a frame of beams and columns. Every beam end and every column end is a hinge that can bend, yield, lose strength and finally give way. That is 440 hinges per frame, set up with published equations calibrated on hundreds of laboratory tests (Haselton and others, 2007).

The weight of the floors is included as it leans. When a building leans, its own weight pushes it further over. Tall frames are sensitive to this, and here it is what brings the tower down.

Every piece of the 3D tower in the video is placed, frame by frame, from this analysis: 4,512 pieces, each following the movement of the joints around it.

The tipping point

Each dot is one run of the whole earthquake at a given strength. Up to about 2.5x, more shaking means a little more movement. Past that, each small step costs much more: 2.5x, 2.6x, 2.7x, 2.8x, 2.9x. At 3x the lower floors can no longer come back and the tower collapses.

5

How it came down

At 3x, the tower survives the strongest part of the shaking. A late jolt, about 27 seconds in, leaves the lower floors leaning. For the next 20 seconds its own weight keeps pulling on that lean. At 47 seconds the lower floors reach a 10 % lean.

That is where the columns fail. A published model for when a damaged concrete column loses its ability to carry load (Elwood and Moehle, 2005) puts it at a 9 to 10 % lean for these columns. Floors 1 to 3 have reached it; floors 4 to 6 are close.

From that moment the analysis stops, and physics takes over. With nothing holding them up, the floors above drop, crush the damaged floors below, and stop on the first undamaged one. The top of the building ends up standing on its own rubble. This part is a simulation for illustration: the joint strengths are calibrated so the undamaged tower stands with the margin real buildings have, but rigid bodies cannot show concrete crushing.

    What this is not

    This is a teaching model. It shows how a designed tower behaves as an earthquake gets stronger. It does not say whether any real building is safe.

    • Each direction is analysed as one flat frame. Twisting and the extra load on corner columns from shaking in both directions at once are left out.
    • One earthquake. A proper collapse study uses 20 or more different recordings, because every earthquake shakes a building differently.
    • The members are sized with a simplified design, not a full one: no bar layouts, no shear design, no joint design.
    • Column strength does not change as the load on the columns shifts during the shaking, and the floor slab's extra strength in the beams is not modelled.
    • After the collapse point, the movement is a physics simulation for illustration, not a structural analysis.
